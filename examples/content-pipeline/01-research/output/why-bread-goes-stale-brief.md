# Why bread goes stale - brief

Written by stage `01-research` on 2026-08-24. This is the only place a fact enters this run. Stage 02 works from the claim table and nothing else.

Question: why does a loaf go hard after a day, when nothing has been taken out of it?

Runtime: 5:00.

## Claim table

| # | Claim | Source | What the source does not say |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Staling is mostly starch retrogradation. Starch molecules that gelatinised during baking slowly recrystallise as the loaf cools and sits. | Food chemistry reference, chapter on cereal starches | It does not put a percentage on how much of staling is retrogradation and how much is moisture movement |
| C2 | A stale loaf has not simply dried out. Bread sealed in a bag, losing almost no water, still goes firm. | Same reference, staling section | It does not describe the bag material or the humidity of the test |
| C3 | Bread stales fastest just above freezing. A fridge is close to the worst temperature for a loaf. | Same reference, staling rate against temperature | It gives a curve, not a single number for a domestic fridge |
| C4 | Freezing a loaf mostly stops staling, because the water is not free to move and the starch cannot recrystallise. | Same reference, frozen storage section | It does not say how many freeze and thaw cycles a loaf survives |
| C5 | Warming stale bread reverses much of the firmness for a short time, then it returns faster than before. | Same reference, reheating section | It does not say how many times this can be repeated |
| C6 | Crust and crumb move in opposite directions. Moisture migrates outward from crumb to crust, so the crumb firms while the crust softens. | Baking science text, chapter on crust | It does not cover loaves with no crust to speak of |

## Open questions

1. How much of everyday staling is retrogradation and how much is water moving around inside the loaf. The sources agree retrogradation dominates and none of them puts a number on the split.
2. Whether a fridge is worse than a warm room for a loaf you will eat tomorrow. The rate curve says yes for retrogradation and says nothing about mould, which is the reason people refrigerate bread in the first place.

Both are carried forward. Neither is smoothed over in the script.
